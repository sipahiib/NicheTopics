# English narration

## Hook

Imagine your photo turning up in an AI generated advert. Could that happen? Yes, in some circumstances. But a similar picture, a training example, and a copyright violation are three different things.

## Distinction

Here is the useful test. Was your image used to train the model? Did the output reproduce your work? And was that use permitted? One answer does not automatically settle the other two.

## Upload

Posting publicly makes an image accessible. It does not, by itself, give everyone permission to use it. Platform terms and the law still matter.

## Meta

Meta says it uses public adult content on its products for AI training in the European Union. It also offers an objection process there. That does not prove your particular photograph was included.

## Regions

Do not assume that the same objection form is available everywhere. Check your account notices and the provider’s current privacy information. Private sharing can reduce exposure, but it cannot recall copies already made.

## Training

A model generally learns patterns across many examples. It is not simply searching a folder of photographs every time you ask for a new image. Yet learning does not guarantee that every detail is forgotten.

## Evidence

Researchers have demonstrated extraction of training images from diffusion models. That establishes a real possibility under studied conditions. It does not mean every model reproduces every photo, or that we tested your account.

## Similarity

Two pictures of an orange umbrella can look alike. A close match in distinctive composition and details deserves closer examination. Resemblance alone does not reveal the training history or settle a legal claim.

## Law

In the United States, the Copyright Office’s training report treats fair use as dependent on the circumstances. It is not a blanket permission slip. Other jurisdictions have different rules. Training and publishing an output raise separate questions.

## Credentials

Content Credentials can carry signed information about an image’s origin and edits. They help you examine its recorded history. They do not block copying, enforce copyright, or prove that everything depicted is true.

## Screenshot

A screenshot or export can separate an image from its embedded credentials. Some systems can reconnect the record using fingerprints or watermarks. A missing badge is not proof of AI generation. A badge is not an ownership verdict.

## Watermark

A visible watermark may discourage casual reuse. It is not a reliable barrier to copying or editing. Keep your original files, dated versions, and publication records instead of trusting a single mark.

## Action

Before sharing valuable work, check visibility, training controls where offered, and the licence terms. Keep an original offline. Avoid uploading client material you are not authorised to share. These steps reduce risk; they cannot guarantee protection.

## End

If you discover a suspicious reuse, save the links and comparison evidence before reporting it. The question is not just, can AI copy a picture? It is, which use did you allow, and what can you prove? Where would you draw the line?
